# Cousin-path failure metrics — informal remittance diversion proxies

**Status:** Public-web research (2026-11-18). No Zimbabwe school-fee specific survey found.  
**Related:** [school-received-confirmation](school-received-confirmation.md) · [demand-mto-diaspora](demand-mto-diaspora.md) · Kuva Cash feedback (open)

The informal alternative to Senda Connect is the cousin path: a diaspora sender sends cash or an EcoCash credit to a relative who is then supposed to pay the bursar. Near-zero fees, but fails on proof and diversion.

## Metrics

| Metric | Figure | Source | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Informal share of remittances (developing countries) | 35–75% of official recorded flows | Freund & Spatafora / World Bank-related; Central Banking; DAI Magister | Sub-Saharan Africa toward the higher end. This is the volume moving via cousins, hand-carry, buses, and informal networks. |
| Remittance stoppages due to recipient misuse | 18.8% of stoppages | University of Birmingham PhD (Ntata, 2024) on Malawian migrants in the UK | 43% of respondents had stopped remitting at some point; 18.8% of those stoppages cited “recipient’s misuse of funds.” Qualitative accounts include school fees and parental support diverted by relatives. |
| Behavioral response to diversion risk | Migrants remit more when they can label or channel funds specifically for education | World Bank lab-in-the-field / EduPay proof-of-concept | The fear of diversion is material enough to change remittance behavior. |

## Working number for the pitch

**~15–20%** of purpose-specific informal sends experience diversion, misuse, or unverifiable receipt.

Combined with the large informal share of total flows, the cousin path has a real, non-trivial failure rate. A Senda fee is not a pure markup if the alternative has a measurable probability of non-delivery or non-proof.

Full Zimbabwe school-fee quantification remains an open interview item (bursars + diaspora parents).
